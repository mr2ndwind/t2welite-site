A 60-minute practice goes fast. Without a plan, it disappears into warm-ups, a couple of drills, and some scrimmaging. With a plan, 60 minutes is enough to develop skills, teach team concepts, and create competitive situations. This guide shows how to structure a productive 60-minute youth basketball practice.

## THE PRACTICE FRAMEWORK

Every practice should include five components. The time you allocate to each depends on the age and level of your team, but the framework stays the same.

- Warm-up and movement preparation (5-8 minutes)
- Skill development (15-20 minutes)
- Team concepts (15-20 minutes)
- Competitive application (10-15 minutes)
- Teaching points and wrap-up (3-5 minutes)

## WARM-UP: 5-8 MINUTES

Start with dynamic movement that prepares the body and reinforces basketball fundamentals. Avoid static stretching at the start of practice — it does not prepare athletes for the explosive movements basketball demands.

- Jogging and lateral shuffles
- Dynamic stretches: lunges, leg swings, arm circles
- Ball handling: stationary dribbling while moving through warm-up
- Form shooting close to the basket

## SKILL DEVELOPMENT: 15-20 MINUTES

This block focuses on individual skill work. Choose one or two skills to emphasize per practice rather than touching everything briefly. Depth beats breadth.

### Sample Skill Block

- 5 minutes: Ball handling (pound dribbles, crossovers, figure-eights)
- 5 minutes: Form shooting and shooting off the catch
- 5-10 minutes: Finishing (layups with both hands, reverse finishes)

> FOR ATHLETES | Quality over quantity. 50 game-speed repetitions with correct technique are more valuable than 200 lazy ones. If athletes are getting sloppy, reduce the volume and raise the standard.

## TEAM CONCEPTS: 15-20 MINUTES

This block teaches team basketball — offensive spacing, defensive positioning, transition, or a specific play. Break concepts into small, teachable pieces. Do not try to install an entire offense in one session.

- Offensive spacing: where to stand and why
- Defensive stance and positioning
- Transition: filling lanes and getting back
- A specific play or set (one per practice, not five)

## COMPETITIVE APPLICATION: 10-15 MINUTES

Apply the skills and concepts in competitive situations. This is where learning becomes instinct. Small-sided games (1v1, 2v2, 3v3) give more touches than 5v5 and force athletes to make decisions.

- 1v1 finishing with a defender
- 3v3 small-sided games with constraints
- 5v5 controlled scrimmage with teaching stops

## TEACHING POINTS AND WRAP-UP: 3-5 MINUTES

End every practice with a brief review. Ask athletes what they learned. Restate the key teaching points. This reinforces learning and helps you assess whether your message landed.

> KEY TAKEAWAY | A 60-minute practice should include warm-up, skill development, team concepts, competitive application, and a teaching wrap-up. Focus on depth over breadth — one or two skills per practice, one team concept, and competitive situations that apply what was taught.
