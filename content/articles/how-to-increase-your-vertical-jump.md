If you play basketball in the Broken Arrow or Tulsa area, one of the most common questions you hear from athletes and parents is simple: how do I jump higher? A bigger vertical jump helps you rebound over taller players, finish at the rim, and contest shots on defense. The good news is that vertical jump is a trainable quality. With consistent, properly progressed training, most athletes can make meaningful gains. This guide breaks down how vertical jump training works and what you can do at home or with a coach to improve it.

## WHAT ACTUALLY DETERMINES YOUR VERTICAL JUMP

Your vertical jump is driven by a combination of factors: lower-body strength, the rate at which you can produce force (called rate of force development), coordination, and body composition. Genetics set the ceiling, but training determines how close you get to it. The athletes who jump the highest are not always the strongest in the weight room. They are the ones who can produce force quickly and coordinate their body efficiently.

### Strength: The Foundation

Before you can produce force quickly, you need the ability to produce force at all. Squats, deadlifts, lunges, and step-ups build the raw strength in your quads, hamstrings, and glutes that every jump relies on. If you have never strength trained before, building a basic foundation of strength is the first step. Many young athletes are surprised how much their vertical improves just from getting stronger.

### Plyometrics: Teaching Your Body to Be Explosive

Plyometric exercises like box jumps, broad jumps, and depth jumps train your muscles and tendons to store and release energy rapidly. This is what makes a jump feel springy rather than sluggish. Plyometrics should be introduced gradually. Jumping to exhaustion or doing high volumes of max-effort jumps on hard surfaces is a fast track to sore knees, not a higher vertical.

> FOR ATHLETES | Quality over quantity. Five explosive, fully-recovered jumps will do more for your vertical than fifty tired ones. Rest between sets so every rep is at full intensity.

## A SIMPLE WEEKLY VERTICAL JUMP TRAINING STRUCTURE

You do not need fancy equipment to start improving your vertical. Here is a simple structure that combines strength and plyometric work. This is general guidance, not a replacement for individualized coaching.

### Two Days Per Week of Lower-Body Strength

- Squat or goblet squat: 3 sets of 5-8 reps
- Romanian deadlift or hip hinge variation: 3 sets of 8 reps
- Walking lunges or split squats: 3 sets of 8 reps per leg
- Calf raises: 3 sets of 12-15 reps

### Two Days Per Week of Plyometrics

- Box jumps: 4 sets of 4 reps (land soft, step down)
- Broad jumps: 4 sets of 3 reps
- Depth drops from a low box: 3 sets of 5 reps
- Single-leg hops in place: 3 sets of 10 per leg

Space your plyometric days apart from your heavy leg days. Your muscles and tendons need 48 hours to recover from high-impact work. Training jump mechanics while fatigued teaches your body bad habits and increases injury risk.

## COMMON MISTAKES THAT HOLD YOUR VERTICAL BACK

- Skipping strength work and only doing plyometrics — you need both
- Jumping on concrete or hard surfaces day after day
- Training the same movement pattern without enough recovery
- Ignoring arm swing, which can add inches to your jump when coordinated properly
- Neglecting core stability, which transfers power from your lower body to the rest of your body

> FOR PARENTS | Young athletes do not need to max out on heavy weights to benefit from strength training. Bodyweight exercises, resistance bands, and light dumbbells build a safe, effective foundation when supervised and progressed properly.

## HOW LONG DOES IT TAKE TO SEE RESULTS?

Most athletes who start a structured program see noticeable improvements in 6 to 8 weeks, with larger gains coming over 3 to 6 months of consistent training. Progress is not linear. You will have weeks where you feel explosive and weeks where you feel flat. That is normal. Track your jump periodically rather than testing it every session.

> KEY TAKEAWAY | Vertical jump is built on a foundation of lower-body strength and developed through properly progressed plyometric training. Consistency and recovery matter more than any single workout.
