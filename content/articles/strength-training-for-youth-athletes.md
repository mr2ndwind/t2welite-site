One of the most common questions we hear from parents in the Broken Arrow and Tulsa area is whether strength training is safe for young athletes. The short answer is yes — when it is supervised, properly progressed, and focused on technique rather than weight. This guide covers what parents need to know about strength training for youth athletes, when to start, and how to make sure it is done safely and effectively.

## IS STRENGTH TRAINING SAFE FOR KIDS AND TEENS?

There is a long-standing myth that strength training stunts growth or is dangerous for young athletes. Current sports medicine guidance says otherwise. Properly supervised resistance training is safe for children and adolescents and can improve strength, coordination, bone health, and confidence. The key word is properly supervised. An unsupervised athlete lifting heavy weights with poor form is at risk regardless of age.

> FOR PARENTS | The risk in youth strength training comes from poor coaching and bad technique, not from the exercises themselves. A qualified coach who emphasizes form over load makes strength training one of the safest activities your athlete can do.

## WHEN SHOULD ATHLETES START STRENGTH TRAINING?

Athletes can begin foundational movement training as early as 8 to 10 years old. At that age, the focus is on bodyweight exercises, balance, and coordination — not lifting heavy weights. Structured strength training with light resistance can begin around 11 to 12, depending on the athlete's maturity and ability to follow coaching cues. By high school, athletes can progressively increase load as their technique and physical development allow.

### Ages 8-10: Movement Foundations

- Bodyweight squats and lunges
- Planks and basic core holds
- Animal crawls and balance work
- Jumping and landing mechanics with soft landings

### Ages 11-13: Introduction to Resistance

- Goblet squats with light dumbbells
- Resistance band rows and pulls
- Dumbbell Romanian deadlifts with light load
- Push-up progressions

### Ages 14+: Progressive Loading

- Barbell squats and deadlifts (when technique is solid)
- Weighted step-ups and lunges
- Pull-ups and rows
- Overhead press with dumbbells or bars

## WHAT A GOOD YOUTH STRENGTH PROGRAM LOOKS LIKE

A well-designed youth strength program prioritizes technique, uses appropriate loads, and progresses gradually. Athletes should never sacrifice form to lift more weight. Sessions should include a proper warm-up, the main strength work, and a cool-down. Two to three sessions per week with at least one day of rest between sessions is appropriate for most youth athletes.

### Signs of a Quality Program

- Coaches teach and correct technique on every set
- Athletes master bodyweight movements before adding load
- Progress is measured by movement quality, not just weight on the bar
- Programs are individualized based on the athlete's age, sport, and experience
- Rest and recovery are built into the schedule

## BENEFITS BEYOND STRENGTH

Strength training does more than make athletes stronger. It improves movement efficiency, which can reduce injury risk. It builds confidence and discipline. And it establishes healthy habits that carry into adulthood. For athletes who play competitive sports, strength training provides a foundation that supports speed, power, and endurance on the court or field.

> FOR ATHLETES | Strength is a skill. Treat every rep as practice, not a test. The athletes who lift with the best technique end up the strongest in the long run because they can train consistently without getting hurt.

> KEY TAKEAWAY | Strength training is safe and beneficial for youth athletes when supervised by a qualified coach and progressed appropriately. Start with movement quality, add resistance gradually, and never rush the process.
