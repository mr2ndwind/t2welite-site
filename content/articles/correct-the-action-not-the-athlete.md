One of the most important principles in coaching is simple but powerful: correct the action, not the athlete. When you address the decision, technique, or behavior, you help the athlete improve. When you attack the person, you create defensiveness and erode trust. This guide explains how to give feedback that athletes can actually use.

## THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN CORRECTING AND CRITICIZING

Correction is teaching. It tells the athlete what happened, what needs to change, and what to do next time. Criticism is judgment. It labels the athlete rather than the action. The difference shows up in the language you use.

- Correction: 'Get below the ball and keep your player and the ball in your vision.'
- Criticism: 'You're not playing defense!'
- Correction: 'Make contact first, find the ball, and then pursue the rebound.'
- Criticism: 'Why didn't you box out?'
- Correction: 'On the next possession, check your balance, spacing, and the time remaining before you shoot.'
- Criticism: 'That was a terrible shot!'

> FOR ATHLETES | Every correction should give the athlete something to do on the next possession. If your feedback only describes what went wrong without offering a solution, it is a complaint, not a correction.

## WHY ATTACKING THE ATHLETE BACKFIRES

When you direct feedback at the athlete as a person — their effort, their character, their intelligence — they stop listening and start defending. Young athletes are still developing emotionally. Personal criticism can damage confidence and motivation, especially in athletes who are already trying their best.

Even when an athlete is not giving full effort, the correction should address the behavior, not the character. 'We need more intensity on closeouts' is more effective than 'You're being lazy.' The first gives a direction. The second creates resentment.

## THE SANDWICH METHOD AND WHEN TO USE IT

The sandwich method — positive, correction, positive — is a well-known feedback tool. It can be effective, but it should be used honestly. If the positive statements are generic or insincere, athletes will tune out the correction too. Use the sandwich when you genuinely have something positive to acknowledge, not as a formula to soften criticism.

## TIMING MATTERS

Corrections are most effective when they come close to the moment, but not always in the middle of a play. During a game, brief cues are better than long explanations. Save detailed teaching for timeouts, substitutions, or practice. In the moment, give the athlete one thing to focus on, not a list of mistakes.

> KEY TAKEAWAY | Correct the action, not the athlete. Address the technique, decision, or behavior with a clear solution for next time. Avoid personal criticism that attacks character or effort. Time your corrections well — brief cues in games, detailed teaching in practice.
