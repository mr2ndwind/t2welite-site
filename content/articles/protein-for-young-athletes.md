Protein is one of the most talked-about nutrients in sports nutrition, and it is also one of the most misunderstood. Athletes and parents often ask how much protein a young athlete needs and whether supplements are necessary. This guide explains the role of protein, common food sources, and why a balanced diet comes before any supplement. This is general education, not individualized medical or nutritional advice.

## WHY PROTEIN MATTERS FOR ATHLETES

Protein plays several important roles in an athlete's body. It supports general growth, which is especially important for young athletes who are still developing. It helps repair tissue damaged during training and competition. And it supports recovery, allowing the body to adapt and get stronger between sessions.

Protein is made up of amino acids, which are the building blocks the body uses for repair and growth. Some amino acids can be produced by the body, while others must come from food. A varied diet that includes protein from different sources generally provides all the amino acids an athlete needs.

## COMMON PROTEIN FOOD SOURCES

The best protein sources are real foods. A food-first approach provides protein along with other important nutrients — vitamins, minerals, and fiber — that supplements do not.

- Eggs
- Greek yogurt and dairy products
- Chicken and turkey
- Fish
- Lean beef
- Beans and lentils
- Nuts and nut butters
- Tofu and soy products

A balanced diet that includes a variety of these sources throughout the day will generally provide enough protein for a young athlete without the need for supplements.

## PROTEIN IS ONLY ONE PART OF NUTRITION

Protein gets a lot of attention, but it is only one component of adequate nutrition. Carbohydrates fuel high-intensity exercise. Fats support hormone production and nutrient absorption. Vitamins and minerals support countless processes in the body. An athlete who eats plenty of protein but not enough carbohydrates will not perform well. Nutrition is about the whole picture, not just one nutrient.

> FOR PARENTS | Do not let protein overshadow the rest of your athlete's nutrition. A balanced plate with carbohydrates, protein, fruits, and vegetables will support performance and recovery better than any single nutrient focus.

## SUPPLEMENTS AS CONVENIENCE, NOT FOUNDATION

Protein supplements — powders, shakes, and bars — can be convenient options in certain situations. An athlete who is rushing from school to practice may benefit from a protein shake. But supplements should be viewed as add-ons to a balanced diet, not replacements for it. A supplement cannot provide the full range of nutrients that real food offers.

If an athlete is eating a balanced diet with adequate protein from food sources, a supplement is unlikely to add meaningful benefit. If an athlete is not eating well, the first step is improving the diet, not adding a supplement.

## HOW TO THINK ABOUT PROTEIN INTAKE

Rather than counting grams, which can be stressful and unnecessary for most young athletes, focus on including a protein source at each meal and snack. This simple habit generally provides enough protein to support growth, repair, and recovery.

- Include a protein source at breakfast (eggs, yogurt, milk)
- Include protein at lunch (chicken, beans, cheese)
- Include protein at dinner (fish, beef, tofu)
- Add a protein-containing snack after training (yogurt, nuts, chocolate milk)

> KEY TAKEAWAY | Protein supports growth, tissue repair, and recovery for young athletes. Get protein from real food sources first — eggs, dairy, poultry, fish, lean meat, beans, and nuts. Supplements are a convenience option, not a foundation. Include a protein source at each meal and focus on balanced nutrition, not just one nutrient.
