Athletic performance training is a term that gets used a lot, but not everyone means the same thing by it. Some programs focus almost entirely on conditioning — making athletes sweat and breathe hard. Others focus on a single quality like speed or strength. A complete athletic performance program develops multiple physical qualities in a structured, progressive way. This guide explains what athletic performance training actually includes and how to recognize a program that is doing it right.

## THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN MAKING ATHLETES TIRED AND TRAINING A QUALITY

There is a big difference between a workout that makes an athlete tired and a session that actually develops a physical quality. Conditioning circuits, high-rep drills, and non-stop movement can create fatigue, but fatigue alone does not produce improvement. Real training targets a specific physical quality — strength, speed, power, agility — and applies the right dose of stimulus to improve it.

A good program alternates between focused work and recovery. It does not pile on volume for the sake of exhaustion. The goal is adaptation, not fatigue.

## WHAT A COMPLETE ATHLETIC PERFORMANCE PROGRAM DEVELOPS

### Strength

Strength is the foundation for almost every other physical quality. It is the ability to produce force. Without adequate strength, speed, power, and agility all have a low ceiling. Strength training for athletes focuses on compound movements — squats, hinges, pushes, pulls — that build functional force production, not bodybuilding.

### Speed

Speed is the ability to move quickly in a straight line. In most sports, the sprints are short, so acceleration matters more than top-end speed. Speed training includes sprint mechanics, acceleration drills, and the strength work that supports force production into the ground.

### Acceleration

Acceleration is the first part of a sprint — how quickly you get from standing still to moving fast. For basketball, football, and soccer players, this is the most sport-relevant part of speed. It is trained through hill sprints, sled pushes, and short explosive starts with an emphasis on body angle and force direction.

### Plyometrics

Plyometrics train the muscles and tendons to store and release energy rapidly. This is what makes athletes explosive and springy. Box jumps, broad jumps, depth jumps, and bounds develop the elastic qualities that translate to jumping higher and changing direction faster.

### Agility

Agility is the ability to change direction quickly and efficiently. It requires deceleration strength, single-leg stability, and body control. Agility is trained through cone drills, shuttle runs, and reactive drills that force athletes to respond to cues rather than memorizing patterns.

### Deceleration

Deceleration is the ability to stop quickly and under control. It is one of the most important and most overlooked qualities in sports. Athletes who cannot decelerate well cannot change direction efficiently and are at higher risk for knee and ankle injuries. Deceleration is trained through landing drills, drop squats, and controlled stops.

### Mobility

Mobility is the ability to move through a full range of motion with control. It is not the same as flexibility. Mobility includes strength and stability at end ranges, which is what athletes actually need. Good mobility supports better movement quality and reduces injury risk.

### Coordination

Coordination is the ability to control your body efficiently. It includes balance, rhythm, and timing. Coordination is developed through varied movement challenges — crawling, skipping, multi-directional drills — that teach the nervous system to organize movement.

### Conditioning

Conditioning is the ability to sustain performance over time. For team sports, this means repeated sprint capacity — the ability to recover between high-intensity efforts. Conditioning should match the energy demands of the sport, not just make athletes tired for the sake of it.

## HOW AGE, EXPERIENCE, AND SPORT SHOULD INFLUENCE PROGRAMMING

A complete program is not one-size-fits-all. A 12-year-old beginner needs a different program than a 17-year-old varsity athlete. Programming should account for training age, biological age, sport demands, and the athlete's individual strengths and weaknesses.

> FOR PARENTS | A good performance coach will assess your athlete before prescribing a program. If a program gives every athlete the same workout regardless of age, experience, or sport, it is not individualized development — it is a fitness class.

## QUESTIONS TO ASK A PERFORMANCE COACH

- What physical qualities does your program develop, and how?
- How do you assess athletes before they start training?
- How do you individualize training for different ages and sports?
- How do you progress athletes safely over time?
- How do you measure progress?
- What is your approach to recovery and injury prevention?

> KEY TAKEAWAY | Athletic performance training is a structured program that develops strength, speed, acceleration, plyometrics, agility, deceleration, mobility, coordination, and conditioning. A quality program targets specific qualities, individualizes based on the athlete, and prioritizes adaptation over exhaustion.
