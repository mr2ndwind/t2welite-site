Soreness vs. Pain: What Parents Should Know

By Coach Corey Scyffore · 3 min read

One of the most common questions parents face when their athlete starts training is how to tell the difference between normal soreness and pain that warrants attention. This is an important distinction. Normal training soreness is expected and harmless. Pain can be a signal that something is wrong. This guide explains the general differences and what to watch for. This is educational information, not medical advice, and is not a substitute for evaluation by a qualified professional.

WHAT NORMAL MUSCLE SORENESS LOOKS LIKE

Muscle soreness after training is normal, especially when an athlete is new to training or has increased intensity or volume. This soreness — often called delayed onset muscle soreness — typically appears 12-24 hours after exercise and peaks at 24-72 hours. It feels like a dull, general ache in the muscles that were worked.

  • Appears 12-24 hours after training
  • Felt in the muscles that were worked
  • Described as a dull ache or tightness
  • Usually affects both sides of the body symmetrically
  • Gradually decreases over 2-4 days
  • Does not significantly alter walking or running mechanics

Normal soreness is part of the adaptation process. It means the muscles were challenged and are recovering. With consistent training, soreness tends to decrease over time as the body adapts.

WARNING SIGNS THAT WARRANT ATTENTION

Certain types of pain are not normal training soreness and may warrant stopping activity and seeking medical evaluation. We are not diagnosing injuries, but these signs should be taken seriously.

  • Sudden, sharp pain during activity
  • Pain that causes a limp or significantly alters walking or running mechanics
  • Significant swelling, redness, or warmth in a joint
  • Pain that persists or worsens beyond 72 hours
  • Pain that is localized to one specific spot rather than a general muscle area
  • Pain that wakes the athlete at night
  • Loss of normal range of motion
  • A popping or snapping sensation at the time of injury
  • Pain that gets worse with continued activity
FOR PARENTSIf your athlete is limping, visibly altering their movement, or experiencing sharp localized pain, stop activity and seek evaluation from an appropriate medical professional. Do not try to push through pain that changes how your athlete moves.

WHEN IN DOUBT, GET IT CHECKED

The safest approach is always to err on the side of caution. If you are unsure whether your athlete is experiencing normal soreness or something more serious, consult a qualified medical professional. Early evaluation can prevent minor issues from becoming major problems.

Attempting to self-diagnose or push through pain that warrants attention can extend recovery time and, in some cases, cause long-term damage. A quick evaluation by a professional is always worth the time.

HOW TO MANAGE NORMAL SORENESS

Normal training soreness can be managed with simple strategies that support recovery.

  • Light movement and activity to promote blood flow
  • Adequate sleep for tissue repair
  • Proper nutrition and hydration
  • Gentle stretching and mobility work
  • Gradual progression of training intensity to allow adaptation

Soreness is not a reason to stop training entirely, but it may be a reason to reduce intensity for a day or two while the body recovers.

KEY TAKEAWAYNormal muscle soreness is a dull, general ache that appears 12-24 hours after training and fades over a few days. Sharp pain, limping, swelling, localized pain, or pain that worsens with activity are warning signs that warrant stopping and seeking medical evaluation. When in doubt, get it checked.
