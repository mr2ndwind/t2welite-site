Hydration for Athletes: What Parents Should Know

By Coach Corey Scyffore · 4 min read

Hydration is one of the most overlooked aspects of athletic performance. By the time an athlete feels thirsty, they are already mildly dehydrated. Dehydration affects energy, focus, reaction time, and can lead to cramping and fatigue. The good news is that hydration is one of the easiest things to get right once you understand the basics. This guide explains what parents and athletes should know about hydration.

HYDRATION IS AN ALL-DAY HABIT

Hydration does not start when the athlete arrives at the gym. It starts the day before and continues throughout the day. Athletes who only drink water during practice are already behind. Good hydration means drinking water consistently throughout the day, every day.

  • Drink water throughout the day before practice or games
  • Sip water in the hours leading up to activity
  • Drink during timeouts, halftime, and breaks
  • Continue hydrating after activity to replace lost fluids

PRACTICES AND GAMES

During practices and games, athletes lose fluids through sweat. The amount depends on the intensity, duration, and environment. Athletes should drink water regularly during activity, not just when they feel thirsty. For sessions lasting longer than an hour or in hot conditions, a sports drink with electrolytes can help replace sodium and other minerals lost through sweat.

TOURNAMENTS AND HOT ENVIRONMENTS

Tournaments present a special challenge. Athletes may play multiple games in a day with limited recovery time. Hydration between games is critical. Encourage athletes to drink water and eat light, hydrating snacks between games. In hot environments, the risk of dehydration increases significantly. Pay extra attention to fluid intake and watch for signs of heat stress.

FOR PARENTSA simple way to check hydration: pale yellow urine usually indicates adequate hydration. Dark yellow urine suggests the athlete needs more fluids. This is a general guide, not a medical diagnostic tool, but it is a useful daily check.

FLUIDS AND ELECTROLYTES

Water is the primary fluid athletes need. For most practices and games, water is sufficient. For longer sessions or hot conditions, electrolytes become important. Electrolytes — particularly sodium — are lost through sweat and need to be replaced during prolonged activity. Sports drinks can provide both fluids and electrolytes, but they are not necessary for every session.

Avoid relying on sugary drinks or energy drinks for hydration. They can cause energy spikes and crashes and are not effective hydration strategies.

INDIVIDUAL NEEDS VARY

Every athlete's hydration needs are different. Sweat rate, body size, environment, and exercise duration all affect how much fluid an athlete needs. We do not give universal fluid prescriptions because what works for one athlete may be too much or too little for another. The best approach is to encourage consistent fluid intake and teach athletes to pay attention to how they feel.

SIGNS THAT SHOULD PROMPT STOPPING ACTIVITY

Certain signs should prompt a parent or coach to stop activity and seek appropriate care. We are not diagnosing medical conditions, but these signs warrant attention.

  • Dizziness or lightheadedness that does not resolve with rest and fluids
  • Confusion or inability to focus
  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Muscle cramps that do not resolve
  • Headache that worsens with activity
  • Stopping or very low sweating when previously sweating in hot conditions

If any of these signs appear, stop activity, move to a cooler environment, provide fluids, and seek appropriate medical attention if symptoms do not improve.

KEY TAKEAWAYHydration is an all-day habit, not a game-time activity. Drink water consistently throughout the day, during activity, and after. For long sessions or hot conditions, electrolytes matter. Individual needs vary, so teach athletes to monitor how they feel and watch for warning signs that warrant stopping activity.
