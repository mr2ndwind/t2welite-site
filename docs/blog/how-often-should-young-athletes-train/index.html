How Often Should Young Athletes Train?

By Coach Corey Scyffore · 5 min read

One of the most common questions parents ask is simple but important: how often should my athlete train? The honest answer is that there is no single number that works for every athlete. The right training volume depends on the athlete's age, training experience, competition schedule, school workload, sleep, and recovery capacity. This guide breaks down how to think about weekly training load so you can build a schedule that supports development instead of undermining it.

UNDERSTANDING THE DIFFERENT TYPES OF TRAINING

Before counting sessions, it helps to understand that not all training is the same. A two-hour team practice and a 45-minute strength session place very different demands on the body. To build a smart week, you need to understand what each type of training does.

  • Team practice: sport-specific skill work, team concepts, and game preparation
  • Games: high-intensity competition that tests current skills under pressure
  • Individual skills: focused, athlete-led development of shooting, ball handling, or position-specific work
  • Strength training: building the force production that supports speed, jumping, and durability
  • Speed and agility training: developing acceleration, deceleration, and change of direction
  • Conditioning: building the aerobic and anaerobic capacity needed to perform late in games
  • Recovery: sleep, nutrition, and rest that allow the body to adapt and improve

TOTAL WEEKLY WORKLOAD MATTERS MORE THAN COUNTING WORKOUTS

A common mistake is counting workouts without considering their intensity and cumulative effect. An athlete who has three team practices, two games, and a tournament on the weekend is already carrying a significant load. Adding three more strength sessions on top of that may be too much — not because strength training is bad, but because the total volume exceeds what the athlete can recover from.

The better question is not how many sessions, but whether the total weekly workload allows for adaptation and recovery. A well-structured week balances high-intensity work with lower-intensity days and includes genuine rest.

FACTORS THAT INFLUENCE HOW MUCH TRAINING IS APPROPRIATE

Athlete Age

Younger athletes recover differently than older athletes. Elementary and early middle school athletes generally benefit from shorter, more varied sessions with more free play. High school athletes can handle more structured volume, but they are also often juggling heavier school loads and more competitive schedules.

Training Experience

An athlete who has been training consistently for two years can handle more volume than one who is just starting. New athletes adapt quickly from less stimulus. Adding too much too soon is one of the fastest ways to create burnout and soreness.

Competition Schedule

During tournament-heavy weekends or playoff stretches, training volume should decrease. The body cannot recover from heavy strength sessions and multiple high-intensity games simultaneously. In-season training is about maintenance and sharpness, not building new capacity.

School Workload and Sleep

School is a stressor. Exam weeks, late-night homework, and early morning practices all affect recovery. An athlete sleeping five hours per night cannot handle the same training load as one sleeping eight. If school demands increase, training volume may need to temporarily decrease.

SIGNS YOUR ATHLETE MAY BE TRAINING TOO MUCH

More training is not always better. When the body is overloaded, it sends signals. Parents and athletes should watch for these signs and take them seriously.

  • Persistent fatigue that does not improve with rest
  • Worsening performance despite consistent training
  • Loss of motivation or enthusiasm for training and competition
  • Abnormal soreness that lingers beyond 48-72 hours
  • Changes in movement quality — looking sluggish, heavy, or uncoordinated
  • Trouble sleeping or changes in mood
FOR PARENTSIf your athlete is showing several of these signs, the answer is not to push harder. Reduce volume, prioritize sleep and nutrition, and give the body time to recover. Overtraining is a real condition that can take weeks or months to correct.

EXAMPLE WEEKLY STRUCTURES

These are general examples, not prescriptions. Every athlete's schedule should be individualized based on their sport, season, and recovery capacity.

Off-Season Middle School Athlete

  • 2 team practices (90 minutes each)
  • 2 individual skill sessions (30 minutes each)
  • 1-2 strength and speed sessions (45 minutes each)
  • 1-2 full rest days

In-Season High School Athlete

  • 4-5 team practices or games
  • 1-2 short individual skill sessions (20-30 minutes)
  • 1 maintenance strength session (30 minutes)
  • 1 full rest day

Tournament Weekend

  • 3-5 games over 2-3 days
  • No additional strength or speed training
  • Light mobility and recovery work between games
  • Full rest day after the tournament
KEY TAKEAWAYThere is no universal number of training sessions that is right for every athlete. Focus on consistent, high-quality development rather than simply adding more workouts. Balance intensity with recovery, and adjust volume based on age, experience, schedule, and how the athlete feels.
