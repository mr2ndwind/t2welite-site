SPEED & AGILITY

Speed and Agility Drills for Basketball Players That Actually Transfer to Games

By Coach Corey Scyffore · 4 min read

Speed in basketball is not the same as running a straight 40-yard dash. On the court, speed means first-step quickness, the ability to stop and change direction on a dime, and the capacity to stay explosive in the fourth quarter. Many athletes spend time on drills that look fast but do not translate to games. This guide focuses on speed and agility training that actually transfers to basketball performance.

WHY BASKETBALL SPEED IS DIFFERENT

In most sports, athletes sprint in a relatively straight line. In basketball, the average sprint is short — often under 15 feet — and almost always involves a change of direction. You accelerate, decelerate, plant, and re-accelerate. This means the muscles and movement patterns that matter most are the ones that control stopping and starting, not top-end speed.

Deceleration Is a Skill

One of the most overlooked aspects of agility is the ability to stop quickly and under control. An athlete who can decelerate fast can change direction sooner and stay balanced. Athletes who cannot decelerate well tend to overshoot, lose their defender for a moment, but then take an extra step to recover — which is often too late.

DRILLS THAT TRANSFER TO THE GAME

1. The 5-10-5 Shuttle

Set up three cones five yards apart. Sprint to the middle cone, touch it, sprint five yards to the end cone, touch it, then sprint ten yards through the far cone. This drill trains acceleration, deceleration, and change of direction in a compact space that mirrors basketball movement.

2. Defensive Slide to Sprint

Start in a defensive stance. Slide laterally for about 10 feet, plant, and immediately sprint forward 10 feet. This mimics closing out on a shooter after helping on defense. The transition from lateral movement to forward sprint is one of the most important movements in basketball.

3. Cone Zig-Zags

Place 5-6 cones in a zig-zag pattern about 10 feet apart. Sprint to each cone, planting your outside foot and changing direction sharply. Focus on low hips and sharp cuts, not drifting wide around the cones.

FOR ATHLETESOn every change of direction, think low and loud. Low hips keep you balanced, and a loud foot strike means you are putting force into the ground instead of sliding across it.

BUILDING THE STRENGTH BEHIND THE SPEED

Drills alone are not enough. The ability to change direction quickly depends on lower-body strength and single-leg stability. Lateral lunges, single-leg squats, and banded lateral walks strengthen the hips and glutes that control side-to-side movement. If your hips are weak, your cuts will be slow no matter how many cone drills you do.

  • Lateral lunges: 3 sets of 8 per leg
  • Single-leg Romanian deadlifts: 3 sets of 8 per leg
  • Banded lateral walks: 3 sets of 10 steps each direction
  • Low box step-ups: 3 sets of 8 per leg

HOW OFTEN SHOULD YOU TRAIN SPEED AND AGILITY?

For most youth and high school athletes, two dedicated speed and agility sessions per week is enough to see improvement without overtraining. These sessions should be short and high-quality. Thirty minutes of focused, intense work beats an hour of half-speed drilling. Always warm up thoroughly before explosive work and give yourself full recovery between sets.

FOR PARENTSSpeed and agility training is safe for young athletes when coached properly. The key is teaching landing and cutting mechanics before adding intensity. A good coach will prioritize movement quality over speed in the early stages.
KEY TAKEAWAYBasketball speed is about acceleration, deceleration, and change of direction — not top-end sprinting. Combine game-like agility drills with lower-body strength work for the best results.
