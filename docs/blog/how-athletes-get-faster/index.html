SPEED & AGILITY

How Athletes Actually Get Faster

By Coach Corey Scyffore · 4 min read

Speed is one of the most valuable physical qualities in sports. It is also one of the most misunderstood. Many athletes and parents assume speed is purely genetic — you either have it or you do not. While genetics set the ceiling, speed is a trainable quality. With the right combination of strength, mechanics, and explosive power development, most athletes can get meaningfully faster. This guide explains how speed development actually works.

WHAT SPEED ACTUALLY IS

Speed is the ability to move your body quickly from one point to another. In sports, we usually care about two phases of speed: acceleration and maximum velocity. Acceleration is how quickly you get moving from a standstill or slow speed. Maximum velocity is your top speed once you are fully sprinting. For most team sports, acceleration matters far more than top-end speed because the sprints are short.

THE PHYSICAL QUALITIES BEHIND SPEED

Force Production

Speed is fundamentally about how much force you can put into the ground and how quickly you can do it. Every sprint step is a force application — pushing against the ground to propel your body forward. The more force you can produce and the faster you can produce it, the faster you will sprint.

Sprint Mechanics

How you move your body while sprinting matters. Good mechanics direct force efficiently into the ground. Poor mechanics waste energy and limit speed. Sprint mechanics include body angle during acceleration, arm action, foot strike, and posture. These can be coached and improved.

Strength

Strength is the foundation of speed. You cannot produce force quickly if you cannot produce force at all. Squats, deadlifts, lunges, and other compound movements build the lower-body strength that every sprint relies on. Many young athletes get faster simply by getting stronger.

Power and Plyometrics

Power is force produced quickly. Plyometric exercises like bounds, jumps, and medicine ball throws train your nervous system to produce force rapidly. This is what makes athletes explosive. Strength without power produces slow strength. Power without strength has a low ceiling. You need both.

Recovery

Speed is a high-intensity quality. It must be trained at full effort with full recovery between repetitions. Sprinting while fatigued does not improve speed — it reinforces slow mechanics. Rest between sprints so every rep is at maximum intensity.

WHY CONDITIONING AND SPEED TRAINING ARE NOT THE SAME

One of the most common mistakes in sports training is confusing conditioning with speed training. Conditioning is about sustaining effort over time. Speed training is about producing maximum effort for a single, short effort. They are trained differently and serve different purposes.

  • Speed training: short sprints (10-30 yards), full recovery (1-3 minutes between reps), maximum intensity
  • Conditioning: longer efforts or short rest, moderate to high intensity, designed to build capacity

If you are sprinting with short rest and feeling tired, you are doing conditioning, not speed training. Both have value, but they are not interchangeable.

FOR ATHLETESSpeed repetitions should prioritize quality. If your last sprint is as fast as your first, you either did not run fast enough or you rested long enough. If your last sprint is much slower than your first, you were doing conditioning, not speed work.

COMMON MISTAKES IN SPEED TRAINING

  • Performing every sprint fatigued — this trains slowness, not speed
  • Skipping strength work and only doing sprints — strength is the foundation
  • Sprinting on hard surfaces without adequate recovery between sessions
  • Focusing on long sprints when most sports demand short acceleration
  • Not coaching or correcting sprint mechanics
  • Treating agility drills as speed training — they are related but different

HOW TO STRUCTURE SPEED TRAINING

For most athletes, two dedicated speed sessions per week is enough to see improvement. Sessions should be short and intense. A typical structure might include a thorough warm-up, 2-3 acceleration drills, 4-6 short sprints with full recovery, and a cool-down. Total sprint volume should be low — quality over quantity.

KEY TAKEAWAYSpeed is a trainable quality built on force production, sprint mechanics, strength, and power. Train it at full intensity with full recovery, and do not confuse it with conditioning. Most team sport athletes need acceleration more than top-end speed.
