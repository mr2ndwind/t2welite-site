When Should Basketball Players Start Thinking About Recruiting?

By Coach Corey Scyffore · 4 min read

One of the most common questions parents ask is when their basketball player should start thinking about recruiting. The answer depends on what we mean by thinking about recruiting. If it means stressing about scholarships, the answer is later. If it means developing the qualities that make an athlete recruitable — skill, academics, character, and film — the answer is now. This guide breaks down what to focus on at each stage.

YOUNGER ATHLETES: FOCUS ON DEVELOPMENT

For elementary and early middle school athletes, recruiting should not be on the radar. The focus should be entirely on development — building skills, athletic literacy, and a love for the game. Athletes at this age benefit from playing multiple sports, developing fundamental movement skills, and learning to enjoy competition.

Parents sometimes feel pressure to start recruiting early, but college coaches are not evaluating 10-year-olds. The best thing you can do for a young athlete's long-term recruiting prospects is to help them fall in love with the game and build a foundation of skills and athleticism.

MIDDLE SCHOOL: BUILD THE FOUNDATION

In middle school, athletes can begin thinking about what it would take to play at the next level, but the focus should still be on development. This is the time to:

  • Develop consistent training habits in skill work and athletic development
  • Begin building academic habits that will matter in high school
  • Play competitive basketball against appropriate competition
  • Start learning about the different levels of college basketball
  • Focus on becoming a better player, not getting recruited

Middle school is not too early to learn about the recruiting landscape, but it is too early to stress about it. Use this time to build the foundation that will matter later.

EARLY HIGH SCHOOL: START THE PROCESS

Freshman and sophomore years are when the recruiting process begins in earnest. This is the time to start being proactive.

  • Prioritize academics — GPA starts counting from freshman year
  • Begin building a highlight reel from game footage
  • Research schools at different levels (Division I, II, III, NAIA, junior college)
  • Start reaching out to coaches at schools that fit your academic and athletic level
  • Play in front of college coaches at appropriate events
  • Continue individual skill and athletic development
FOR PARENTSAcademics are part of recruiting from the first day of freshman year. A strong GPA gives your athlete options at every level. A weak GPA takes options away, even for talented players. Help your athlete understand this early.

LATER HIGH SCHOOL: BE PROACTIVE

Junior and senior years are when recruiting becomes more active. College coaches are evaluating, communicating, and making decisions. Athletes need to be proactive.

  • Update and send highlight film to coaches at target schools
  • Maintain academic eligibility — this is non-negotiable
  • Attend camps and showcases where coaches from your level are present
  • Communicate directly with coaches — express interest and follow up
  • Be realistic about your level and find the right fit
  • Keep developing — coaches recruit based on trajectory, not just current ability

DEVELOPMENT BEFORE EXPOSURE

The most important principle in recruiting is that development comes before exposure. Exposure without development does not lead to recruitment — it leads to being seen and passed over. The athletes who get recruited are the ones who have developed the skills, athleticism, academics, and character that college coaches want.

Do not chase exposure before you are ready. Use your time to become a player worth finding. When you are good enough, the exposure will matter. Until then, it is just noise.

FINDING THE RIGHT LEVEL

College basketball has multiple levels, and most athletes will not play Division I. That is not a failure — it is reality. Division II, Division III, NAIA, and junior college all offer opportunities to play college basketball and get an education. The goal is to find the level where the athlete can contribute, get playing time, and thrive academically and socially.

Be honest about your ability. A player who is a strong contributor at the Division III level will have a better experience than one who sits on the bench at Division I. Finding the right fit matters more than the division label.

KEY TAKEAWAYRecruiting is a process that spans years. Young athletes should focus on development. Middle schoolers build the foundation. Early high schoolers start being proactive. Later high schoolers communicate with coaches and find the right level. Development always comes before exposure, and finding the right fit matters more than the division label.
