SPEED & AGILITY

Agility vs. Speed: What Athletes and Parents Should Know

By Coach Corey Scyffore · 4 min read

Speed, change of direction, and agility are often used interchangeably, but they are not the same thing. Understanding the difference matters because each quality is trained differently and contributes differently to sports performance. This guide explains what each one is and why athletes need all three.

DEFINING THE THREE QUALITIES

Speed

Speed is how fast you move in a straight line from point A to point B. It is the simplest of the three qualities. In sports, we usually care about acceleration — the first 10-20 yards — more than top-end speed because most sprints in team sports are short.

Change of Direction

Change of direction is the ability to stop and redirect your movement. This is a planned movement — you know where you are going. Cone drills, shuttle runs, and zig-zag drills train change of direction. It requires deceleration strength, single-leg stability, and the ability to re-accelerate after a direction change.

Agility

Agility is the ability to change direction in response to a stimulus. This is a reactive movement — you do not know what you will do until you see what is happening. Defending a ball handler, reacting to a pass, or recovering to help on defense all require agility. Agility combines change of direction with perception and decision making.

WHY MEMORIZING CONE PATTERNS IS NOT ENOUGH

Many programs train change of direction but call it agility. Athletes memorize cone patterns and run them at high speed. This has value — it develops the physical ability to change direction. But it does not fully train agility because there is no reaction involved. In games, athletes rarely know exactly where they will move next. They react to the ball, opponents, and the situation.

True agility training includes a reactive element. A coach holds up a cone, points a direction, or throws a ball, and the athlete responds. This trains the perception and decision making that separate athletes who test well in drills from athletes who play well in games.

FOR ATHLETESIf you can run a cone drill perfectly but get beat on defense, your issue is not change of direction — it is agility. You need reactive drills that force you to read and respond, not just memorized patterns.

BASKETBALL EXAMPLES OF EACH QUALITY

  • Speed: sprinting downcourt on a fast break
  • Change of direction: running a cone zig-zag drill in training
  • Agility: staying in front of a ball handler who is reading your positioning and reacting to your moves
  • Agility: closing out on a shooter, then reacting to whether they shoot, drive, or pass
  • Agility: recovering to help defense, then reacting to where the ball goes next

WHAT QUALITY AGILITY COACHING SHOULD INCLUDE

Parents evaluating a speed and agility program should look for more than cone drills. Quality coaching includes:

  • Teaching deceleration and landing mechanics before adding intensity
  • Combining planned change of direction drills with reactive agility work
  • Using visual and verbal cues to force athletes to read and respond
  • Connecting agility work to sport-specific situations
  • Progressing difficulty gradually as athletes improve
  • Prioritizing movement quality over drill speed in the early stages
FOR PARENTSA good agility program will not just make your athlete tired. It will make them quicker and more reactive. Ask the coach how they incorporate reaction and decision making into their drills, not just how fast athletes run through cones.
KEY TAKEAWAYSpeed is straight-line movement. Change of direction is planned redirection. Agility is reactive redirection based on a stimulus. Athletes need all three, and true agility training includes a reactive element that memorized cone drills do not provide.
