Sleep: The Athlete's Underrated Performance Tool

By Coach Corey Scyffore · 4 min read

Sleep is the most underrated performance tool available to athletes. Training provides the stimulus for improvement, but recovery is where adaptation actually happens. Without adequate sleep, the body cannot fully adapt to training, and performance suffers. This guide explains why sleep matters for athletes and how to build better sleep habits.

WHY RECOVERY BELONGS IN AN ATHLETE DEVELOPMENT PLAN

Many athletes and parents focus entirely on training — more sessions, more intensity, more work. But training is only half the equation. The body improves during recovery, not during the workout itself. Sleep is the most important recovery tool an athlete has. During sleep, the body repairs tissue, consolidates learning, regulates hormones, and prepares for the next day.

Athletes who consistently sleep well recover faster, perform better, and are less likely to get injured. Athletes who do not sleep enough plateau, feel fatigued, and are more susceptible to illness and injury.

HOW MODERN SCHEDULES WORK AGAINST SLEEP

Young athletes face real challenges to getting enough sleep. School starts early. Practices run late into the evening. Tournaments require travel and early mornings. Homework piles up. And screens — phones, tablets, and computers — keep athletes awake longer than they realize.

  • Early school start times cut sleep short
  • Evening practices push bedtime later
  • Tournament travel disrupts sleep schedules
  • Homework and studying extend into the night
  • Screen time before bed delays sleep onset
  • Irregular weekend schedules disrupt the body's rhythm

HOW ATHLETES AND PARENTS CAN CREATE BETTER ROUTINES

Better sleep does not happen by accident. It requires intentional habits and routines. Here are practical steps athletes and parents can take.

Set a Consistent Sleep Schedule

Going to bed and waking up at the same time every day — even on weekends — helps regulate the body's internal clock. Inconsistent schedules make it harder to fall asleep and reduce sleep quality.

Limit Screen Time Before Bed

The blue light from screens suppresses melatonin, the hormone that signals sleep. Aim to stop screen use 30-60 minutes before bed. If that is not realistic, use a blue light filter and keep screens out of the bedroom.

Create a Wind-Down Routine

A consistent routine before bed signals the body that it is time to sleep. This might include stretching, reading, or listening to calm music. Avoid intense activity, heavy meals, and stressful conversations right before bed.

Prioritize Sleep During Tournament Weekends

Tournaments are sleep's biggest enemy. Early games, travel, and hotel stays all disrupt sleep. Do what you can to protect sleep — choose earlier bedtimes, limit screen time in hotels, and encourage naps between games if the schedule allows.

FOR PARENTSHelp your athlete understand that sleep is part of training, not a break from it. The athletes who recover best are the ones who sleep consistently. Protect sleep like you protect practice time.

MORE TRAINING IS NOT ALWAYS MORE DEVELOPMENT

There is a common belief that more training always produces more development. But if training increases while sleep stays the same or decreases, the athlete is adding stimulus without adding recovery. The result is not more development — it is accumulated fatigue, reduced performance, and increased injury risk.

Before adding more training to an athlete's schedule, ask whether their recovery can support it. If an athlete is already sleeping poorly, adding more workouts will not help them improve. It will make things worse.

KEY TAKEAWAYSleep is the most important recovery tool an athlete has. The body adapts during recovery, not during training. Build consistent sleep routines, limit screen time before bed, and protect sleep during tournaments. More training without adequate recovery does not produce more development.
